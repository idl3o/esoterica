# The Death of Baldr

## Where the Story Is Kept

The death of Baldr is told in full in one place: the prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson, an Icelandic book of around 1220, written two centuries after Iceland accepted Christianity. The older poems allude to the story without telling it. A seeress in Voluspa sees the mistletoe and the shot. A poem called Baldr's Dreams has Odin ride down to the dead to learn what the dreams mean. In Lokasenna, Loki boasts of the killing to Frigg's face. A skald named Ulf Uggason described the funeral as it was carved on the panels of a house. And at the end of the twelfth century the Danish historian Saxo Grammaticus told a different story altogether, in which Baldr is a demigod and his killer a man with a sword.

Four earlier films in this series were made from essays on Baldr, on the blind god Hodr, on the giantess Thokk and on Sigyn, and they read the story as a teaching about innocence, about a light that never reached the dark, about staying beside what is bound. Set against the sources, many of the details those readings rested on turned out to be Snorri's alone, or a modern translator's, or nobody's, and the readings had left out the vengeance. Here the story is told as the sources tell it, layer by layer, with the readings named as readings.

## The Dreams

The oldest telling begins with the gods in council.

SOURCE. Once were the gods together met,
And the goddesses came and council held,
And the far-famed ones the truth would find,
Why baleful dreams to Baldr had come.

That is Baldr's Dreams, in Henry Adams Bellows' translation of 1923. The poem never says what the dreams were. Odin saddles Sleipnir and rides down towards the dead, past a hound with blood on its breast, to the eastern door where a wise-woman lies buried. He raises her with spells. She complains of the grave's weather, snow and rain and dew, and of being long dead. He gives a false name and asks who the benches bright with rings are for. She answers that the mead is brewed for Baldr. He asks who will kill him.

SOURCE. Hoth thither bears the far-famed branch,
He shall the bane of Baldr become,
And steal the life from Othin's son.
Unwilling I spake, and now would be still.

Odin does not let her be still. His next question is who will take vengeance.

SOURCE. Rind bears Vali in Vestrsalir,
And one night old fights Othin's son;
His hands he shall wash not, his hair he shall comb not,
Till the slayer of Baldr he brings to the flames.

In the oldest account the story already has its shape: a dream, a killer, and a brother born to kill the killer. Loki is not at the shot. He appears only in the seeress's last words, when she refuses to be questioned again "Till Loki wanders loose from his bonds".

## The Oath and the Sprout

Snorri's prose supplies what the poems leave out, and most of what the later readings used.

When Baldr told the gods his dreams, they decided to ask safety for him from every kind of danger, and Frigg took oaths from fire and water, iron and every metal, stones, earth, trees, sicknesses, beasts, birds, venom and serpents. Then the gods made a game of it. Baldr stood in the assembly while the others shot at him, hewed at him and threw stones, and nothing hurt him.

Loki went to Frigg's hall, Fensalir, in the likeness of a woman, and asked her.

SOURCE. Then the woman asked: 'Have all things taken oaths to spare Baldr?' and Frigg answered: 'There grows a tree-sprout alone westward of Valhall: it is called Mistletoe; I thought it too young to ask the oath of.'

That is Arthur Gilchrist Brodeur's translation of 1916. Elsewhere Snorri says that Frigg knows all the fates of men, though she speaks no prophecy. Here she speaks, and to a stranger. The one thing that will kill her son is the one thing she did not treat as a danger, and the one thing she tells.

Why too young? The usual answer is that the sprout seemed too small to matter. Merrill Kaplan has argued for a legal reason: young people were not eligible to swear legal oaths, which could make them a threat later in life. The mistletoe of Voluspa is not small. The seeress sees it standing "Full grown in strength", higher than the fields, slender and very fair.

## The Shot

SOURCE. Hödr stood outside the ring of men, because he was blind. Then spake Loki to him: 'Why dost thou not shoot at Baldr?' He answered: 'Because I see not where Baldr is; and for this also, that I am weaponless.'

Loki tells him to honour Baldr as the others do, and says he will direct him where Baldr stands. Hodr shoots the mistletoe, "being guided by Loki", and Baldr falls dead. In Brodeur's words it was "the greatest mischance that has ever befallen among gods and men".

Three details here have carried much of the later weight. The first is the guided hand. Snorri says Loki directed Hodr where to aim. A hand guided at the wrist first appears, among the texts read for this film, in a note Bellows wrote in 1923. The second is the blindness. Snorri states it twice and has the poets call Hodr the Blind God, but the older poems do not mention it, Saxo's Hother is a strong swimmer and a skilled archer, and skalds used Hodr's name in kennings for warriors. The third is innocence. Snorri gives Hodr no motive at all. A reader may infer innocence from the deception; the text does not state it.

What the text does give is the gods' response.

SOURCE. Then, when Baldr was fallen, words failed all the Æsir, and their hands likewise to lay hold of him; each looked at the other, and all were of one mind as to him who had wrought the work, but none might take vengeance, so great a sanctuary was in that place.

They knew who had done it, and they held back because of where they stood. The vengeance was deferred. It was not refused.

## The Ship and the Ride

The gods brought Baldr's body to the sea. His ship, Hringhorni, was the greatest of all ships, and it would not move off its rollers. They sent to the giants' country for a giantess called Hyrrokkin, who came riding a wolf with a viper for a bridle; four berserks had to fell the wolf to hold it. She thrust the ship out at the first push, so that fire burst from the rollers and all lands trembled, and Thor gripped his hammer and would have killed her if the gods had not asked peace for her.

When Baldr's wife Nanna saw the body carried on board, her heart burst with grief, and she was borne to the pyre beside him. Thor hallowed the pyre with his hammer and kicked a dwarf named Litr into the fire. Odin came with Frigg, the valkyries and his ravens; Freyr in a chariot drawn by a boar; Heimdall on a horse called Gold-Top; Freyja with her cats; and frost-giants and hill-giants. Odin laid on the pyre the gold ring Draupnir, from which eight rings of equal weight dropped every ninth night, and Baldr's horse was led to the fire with all its trappings.

The ship is not said to have sailed. One of the earlier films had it burning out to sea. In Snorri, Hyrrokkin pushes it off the rollers where the gods could not, the body is carried on board, and the fire is kindled. Ulf Uggason's poem, which Snorri quotes, describes the same funeral as scenes carved in a house, and its refrain says so.

SOURCE. There I think the Valkyrs follow,
And ravens, Victorious Odin
To the blood of holy Baldr.
With old tales the hall was painted.

One moment of the funeral is beyond recovery. The poem Vafthrudnismal ends with Odin putting a last question to the giant Vafthrudnir, who has answered every other.

SOURCE. What spake Othin himself in the ears of his son,
Ere in the bale-fire he burned?

The giant cannot answer it. Only the one who spoke the words knows them, and the question gives the questioner away.

Frigg asked who would ride to Hel to ransom Baldr, and Odin's son Hermod took Sleipnir. He rode nine nights through dark, deep dales until he came to the river Gjoll and its bridge, thatched with glittering gold, and the maiden who guards it.

SOURCE. Módgudr is the maiden called who guards the bridge; she asked him his name and race, saying that the day before there had ridden over the bridge five companies of dead men; but the bridge thunders no less under thee alone, and thou hast not the color of dead men.

The thunder is under Hermod, the living rider. One of the earlier films gave it to Baldr, as the sound of his crossing. The text gives Baldr's crossing no sound at all. Matthew Arnold, retelling the scene in 1855, kept it right.

Hermod leapt the gate of Hel and found Baldr sitting in the high seat. Hel set her condition.

SOURCE. If all things in the world, quick and dead, weep for him, then he shall go back to the Æsir; but he shall remain with Hel if any gainsay it or will not weep.

Baldr sent the ring Draupnir back to Odin as a remembrance. Nanna sent Frigg a linen smock.

## The Weeping and Thokk

The gods sent messengers through the whole world, and everything wept: men, living things, the earth, stones, trees and metals, weeping as such things do, Snorri says, when they come out of frost into heat. On their way home the messengers found a giantess sitting in a cave, who called herself Thokk.

SOURCE. Thökk will weep
waterless tears
For Baldr's bale-fare;
Living or dead,
I loved not the churl's son;
Let Hel hold to that she hath!

Her name means "thanks". Her verse answers Hel's condition in its own terms: Hel asked that all things weep, quick and dead, and Thokk refuses, living or dead. In the Old Norse her complaint is that Baldr was of no use to her. Brodeur's churl's son is contempt.

Then Snorri adds one sentence, and its exact wording matters.

SOURCE. And men deem that she who was there was Loki Laufeyarson, who hath wrought most ill among the Æsir.

The Old Norse reads en thess geta menn: people guess, or suppose. Snorri is the only source to make the identification, and he reports it as a supposition. One of the earlier films quoted an Old Norse phrase meaning "people hold it for true that this was Loki". That phrase is not in the text.

The same film read Thokk's refusal as accuracy: Baldr's light had never reached her cave, and her dry tears were the one honest testimony in creation. It is a reading, and it may be a good one. The verse gives her reason as personal, and Snorri gives her identity as a guess. Nothing in the source speaks of light, or of its reach.

## Vali

Vali is the figure the earlier readings left out. The essay on Hodr said that the myth refuses to punish him: a justice myth would exile or mark the blind killer, and the Norse cosmology does the opposite. Every older source says otherwise.

Baldr's Dreams has Vali born to Rind for the purpose, fighting one night old, his hands unwashed and his hair uncombed until he brings Baldr's slayer to the flames. Voluspa repeats it.

SOURCE. But the brother of Baldr was born ere long,
And one night old fought Othin's son.

SOURCE. His hands he washed not, his hair he combed not,
Till he bore to the bale-blaze Baldr's foe.

Snorri's own handbook for poets confirms it. Among the ways to name Vali are "Baldr's Avenger, Foe and Slayer of Hödr". In Saxo's version Odin begets a son on Rinda to avenge Balder, and that son, Bous, kills Hother in battle.

Hodr is in Hel, to return from it after the end of the world, because he was killed. John Lindow's study of the Baldr story, published in 1997, reads it as a story of feud. In his publisher's words, Baldr's story "does not end with his death but always involves acts of vengeance". His last chapter places reconciliation after the end of the world, as the last of four stages: conflict, climax, revenge and reconciliation. The earlier film kept the fourth stage and left out the third.

## The Binding and the Bowl

Snorri makes Loki's punishment the payment for Baldr. Asked whether any vengeance was taken on Loki, the narrator answers that it was repaid him in such a way that he will remember it long.

SOURCE. Now Loki was taken truceless, and was brought with them into a certain cave. Thereupon they took three flat stones, and set them on edge and drilled a hole in each stone.

A son of Loki's, also named Vali, was turned into a wolf and tore apart his brother Narfi, and the gods bound Loki with the entrails over the three stones, and the bonds turned to iron. The giantess Skadi fastened a venomous serpent above his face.

SOURCE. But Sigyn, his wife, stands near him and holds a basin under the venom-drops; and when the basin is full, she goes and pours out the venom, but in the meantime the venom drips into his face. Then he writhes against it with such force that all the earth trembles: ye call that 'earthquakes.'

The bowl is attested in two prose passages: this one, and the prose that closes the poem Lokasenna, which calls it a shell, gives the roles of Loki's two sons the other way round, and does not tie the binding to Baldr at all. Bellows thought the compiler had attached the punishment to the wrong incident. In the poems Sigyn appears once, in Voluspa, sitting by the bound one.

SOURCE. By his side does Sigyn sit, nor is glad
To see her mate: would you know yet more?

She is older than the bowl. A ninth-century skaldic poem calls Loki "the Arm-Burden then of Sigyn", and a carving on the Gosforth Cross in Cumbria, from the eleventh century, has been read as her: a kneeling woman holding something above a bound figure, with a knotted serpent nearby.

The earlier film on Sigyn built a practice from her: three refusals, not to free him, not to approve, not to leave, and the claim that the only bound thing that can ever be safely released is one that was never abandoned in its binding. The sources give her one line of feeling, that she is not glad. And they supply the counter-case. Loki was not abandoned in his binding; Sigyn stays by him. At the end of the world he stands at the helm of a ship carrying the people of Hel, and he and Heimdall kill each other. Whatever the practice is worth, the myth it was drawn from runs the other way.

## Saxo's Balder

The Danish version shares a name and an invulnerability with Snorri's, and little else. Saxo, writing at the end of the twelfth century, tells it as history. Hother is a human prince, strong, a swimmer and an archer, skilled on the lyre and the harp. Balder, a demigod whose body common steel could not wound, sees Nanna bathing and resolves to kill Hother to have her.

SOURCE. For he said that the sacred strength of Balder's body was proof even against steel; adding, however, that he knew of a sword which could deal him his death, which was fastened up in the closest bonds; this was in the keeping of Miming, the Satyr of the woods

The rest is war. Hother wins the sword. In a sea battle where Odin and Thor fight for Balder, Hother hews off Thor's club at the haft. Three snakes drip venom into the food that keeps Balder strong. Hother meets him and plunges the sword into his side, and Balder, carried into battle on a litter, sees Proserpine standing by him in a dream and dies after three days. Odin then begets a son on Rinda to avenge him.

The introduction to the English translation asks whether Miming's sword was the mistletoe. There is no telling. The two stories share an exception to invulnerability, and in both the killing is avenged.

## After the Burning

Voluspa ends with the earth rising green from the sea.

SOURCE. Then fields unsowed bear ripened fruit,
All ills grow better, and Baldr comes back;
Baldr and Hoth dwell in Hropt's battle-hall,
And the mighty gods: would you know yet more?

Snorri says the same in prose. After the burning, Baldr and Hodr come from Hel, and all sit down together and talk, and call to mind the Midgard Serpent and the wolf, and find in the grass the golden chess-pieces the gods had once owned.

Neither source uses the word reconciled. Bellows, in a note, called the brothers' union a symbol of the new age of peace, and Lindow ends his book on reconciliation; both place it after the vengeance, not instead of it. Nor is the new world without judgment. The same poem sees a hall far from the sun on Nastrond, wound about with serpents, where treacherous men and murderers wade through rivers, and a hall roofed with gold at Gimle, where the righteous dwell. And one more poem, Vafthrudnismal, names the gods who survive the end. Baldr and Hodr are not among them.

## What Is Declined

No source lets Hodr go unpunished. Vali kills him in every early telling, and Snorri's gods hold back only because of where they stand.

No source ends the world without judgment either. "All ills grow better" stands in the same poem as Nastrond and Gimle.

Four details given as the text have no text behind them: a ship sailing out burning, the thunder of Baldr's crossing, a hand guided at Hodr's wrist, and an Old Norse sentence Snorri did not write. Nor is Thokk known to be Loki; Snorri reports a supposition.

The likeness between Baldr and Christ is real and long noticed, and it is offered here as an analogy, not as a structural identity. The fullest Baldr story was written in Christian Iceland, some scholars find Christian influence in Voluspa, and Sophus Bugge held that nearly all the Old Norse myths derive from Christian and late classical concepts. His thesis was generally rejected, yet it has had influence, and while derivation remains possible a resemblance cannot count as two independent witnesses.

The readings stay, as readings: the light that did not reach Thokk's cave, the innocence of the blind instrument, the bowl as a refusal to abandon. They are readings of a thirteenth-century Icelandic book and of its modern retellings. And one question stays open, because the sources leave it so. What Odin said into his son's ear at the pyre, none of them records.
