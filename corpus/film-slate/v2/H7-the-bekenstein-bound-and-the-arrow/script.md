# The Bekenstein Bound and the Arrow

## The Box on the String

In 1981 Jacob Bekenstein published an inequality about boxes. Take any system that fits inside a sphere of radius R, and count its total energy, every part of it, the walls included. Its entropy, which counts the states the system could be in, cannot exceed two pi times that energy times that radius, divided by Planck's constant and the speed of light.

The argument behind it was a thought experiment. Lower a box of matter on a string, slowly, towards the horizon of a large black hole, and let it go. The box's entropy leaves the outside world. If the generalised second law holds, the black hole has to make up the loss, and its entropy grows with the area of its horizon. Work out how far a box of that size and that energy can enlarge the horizon, and a limit on the box follows.

SOURCE. Black holes also comply with the bound; in fact they actually attain it. Thus, as long suspected, black holes have the maximum entropy for given mass and size which is allowed by quantum theory and general relativity.

There is no area in the inequality. There is a radius and an energy, and Newton's constant does not appear. Raphael Bousso, writing for the volume published in Bekenstein's memory, put it plainly.

SOURCE. The Bekenstein bound is purely a nongravitational statement, and it is intrinsically about the matter system itself: the first universal entropy bound.

This film replaces an earlier one in the series. The earlier film told the history of the bound with two additions: that its rigorous form gives a surface an arrow of time, and that the proof cannot be finished until an observer is adjoined. For this film the papers were read again, in the physicists' own words. Neither addition survives that reading. What follows is the history as they tell it, then the arrow and the observer, each placed where the literature places them.

## Area and Energy

A different sentence has travelled further than the inequality: that the information a region can hold is proportional to the area of its surface, not its volume. It has two sources, and neither is the Bekenstein bound.

The first is the entropy of a black hole. Bekenstein proposed in 1972 and 1973 that a black hole carries entropy in proportion to the area of its horizon, and Stephen Hawking fixed the constant in 1975: a quarter of the area, in units of the Planck length squared. That is an equality, and it is about black holes.

The second is the holographic principle. In 1993 Gerard 't Hooft argued that the degrees of freedom of quantum gravity could be described as if they were Boolean variables defined on a two-dimensional lattice, and Leonard Susskind gave the idea its picture: the three-dimensional world as an image of data stored on a two-dimensional surface, one discrete degree of freedom per Planck area. The name came from optics.

SOURCE. Holography is an optical technology by which a three-dimensional image is stored on a two-dimensional surface via a diffraction pattern. (To avoid any confusion: this linguistic remark will remain our only usage of the term in its original sense.)

An optical hologram has a property the physics does not borrow: cut the plate, and each piece still shows the whole scene. The principle claims something narrower: that the data of a region can be written on a surface, at a fixed density. It is a conjecture. Bousso's review calls the relation between geometry and information universal, and adds that it "has yet to be explained".

The simplest version of the area claim fails. Bousso's review of 2002 tested the statement that the entropy inside any spatial volume is less than the area of its boundary, and found it violated by four ordinary systems. His verdict was that the idea that the area of surfaces generally bounds the entropy in enclosed spatial volumes "has proven wrong". The version that survives is his own covariant bound of 1999. From a two-dimensional surface, four families of light rays leave at right angles, two towards the future and two towards the past, one pair to each side. Follow any family whose rays are not spreading apart, for as long as they do not spread, and the sheet they sweep out is a light-sheet. The entropy on any light-sheet does not exceed a quarter of the surface's area. It is a conjecture. It has been proved classically under conditions on how entropy flows, and for free quantum fields where gravity is weak.

The two kinds of bound are distinct, and they are also linked. Where both apply, the Bekenstein bound is the stronger: add the condition that a sphere of matter is stable against collapse, and Bekenstein's inequality gives the area bound as a consequence. Going the other way, Bousso showed in 2003 that his covariant bound implies a version of Bekenstein's in weak gravity.

SOURCE. Bekenstein's original 1981 bound did not stand logically apart from the "holographic" entropy bound I had found much later. Instead, it corresponded to a particular weakly gravitating limit of a more general, gravitational bound.

Neither bound comes near anything on a human scale. The area bound allows about ten to the sixty-nine bits for every square metre. Of the energy bound, Bekenstein wrote that the entropies of mundane systems "fall well below" it. A brain, a soil or a data centre is a mundane system in that sense, nowhere near either limit.

## Thirty Years of Argument

The inequality was not quietly accepted. In 2000 Don Page published systems that, he said, could violate it "by an arbitrarily large factor": a scalar field with domain walls, the electromagnetic modes between a great many conducting plates, and the modes in a coaxial cable. Bekenstein replied the same year that the energies of all essential parts of the system must be included, the plates and the cable and anything holding them in place. The reply worked, at a price that Bousso named.

SOURCE. However, in forcing us to include the rest energy of enclosures, Bekenstein risked trivializing his bound, by making it virtually impossible to find a system that would come close to saturating it.

The hardest case was the species problem. Suppose a photon could come in any of a great many species, and prepare one in a mixture of all of them. In Bousso's words, “we know that a photon wavepacket is present, but not which species it was made from”. Its entropy then grows with the number of species while its energy stays the same, and with enough species the bound breaks.

There was a second argument about whether the bound was needed at all. William Unruh and Robert Wald argued that quantum field theory already protects the generalised second law without it. Decades later Bousso conceded that Unruh and Wald "were right", and that in this sense the bound had not been needed.

Bousso's memorial essay names what the argument should have been about: how to define the entropy, the energy and the radius in the first place. A quantum system is rarely sharply localised, so its radius is not obvious, and his own attempts to fix that went nowhere.

SOURCE. So, in a set of deservedly forgotten papers, I pursued various other approaches to sharpening the formulation of the Bekenstein bound.

His aim, he wrote, had been “to formulate the Bekenstein bound in a way that was neither wrong, nor trivial, nor ill-defined”.

In the same essay he records that Bekenstein, in conversation, was characteristically low-key, and called the bound a "rule of thumb".

## The Difference from the Vacuum

The answer came in two steps, and neither of them belongs to the earlier film's version of the story.

In 2003 Donald Marolf, Djordje Minic and Simon Ross looked at the species problem from the side of an accelerated observer near a horizon, and found that the entropy such an observer assigns stays bounded however many species there are. They called entropy, in this setting, observer-dependent. In 2008 Horacio Casini built on their work. He did not ask for the entropy of a region. In quantum field theory that entropy is infinite, because the field is entangled across the region's edge at every scale. He asked for a difference: the entropy of a state in the region, minus the entropy of the vacuum in the same region. Both are infinite; the difference is finite. On the energy side he put the matching difference in the region's modular energy, a quantity fixed by the vacuum itself. For half of space, the side of a flat wall, that modular energy is a precise integral, and for a small object far from the wall it comes to two pi times energy times distance, the product Bekenstein had written down. At the centre of a sphere, in a conformal theory, it comes to about half of that.

The inequality then states that the entropy difference cannot exceed the modular energy difference, and the gap between them is the relative entropy of the state with respect to the vacuum: a measure of how distinguishable two states are, and the quantum relative of the Kullback–Leibler divergence of classical information theory. Relative entropy is never negative. So the bound holds. Bousso's summary is that Casini then proved it "in one line".

SOURCE. In this formulation the bound holds automatically, and in particular it does not suffer from the proliferation of the species problem. The results suggest that while the bound is relevant at the classical level, it does not introduce new physical constraints semiclassically.

That is Casini's own abstract, and the second sentence carries the weight. In this form the bound is not a new law laid on quantum fields. It follows from properties quantum fields already have. The species problem dissolves for a plain reason: as species are added, the vacuum in the region becomes more entropic too, so the difference between the state and the vacuum stops growing.

The relative entropy itself needed no repair. Casini noted that in quantum field theory it is "perfectly well defined, independently of any cutoff procedure", by a formula of Huzihiro Araki's. What lacked a clean definition were the two terms taken separately. Roberto Longo and Feng Xu gave an operator-algebraic derivation of the bound in 2018. Then, in a preprint of 2023 published in 2025, Jonah Kudler-Flam and four colleagues defined each side on its own. Their tool is the modular crossed product: they enlarge the space of states by square-integrable wavefunctions on the real line, which gives finite, renormalised entropies, and they describe it as "a useful tool in regulating (non-gravitational) QFT". Their stated improvement on Casini is "demonstrating that both sides of this expression can be independently well-defined".

Casini's vacuum subtraction, without any crossed product, reached the other bound too. In 2014 Bousso, Casini, Zachary Fisher and Juan Maldacena proved a quantum version of the covariant bound, again with vacuum-subtracted entropies, for free fields where gravity is weak. The energy bound and the area bound have proofs of the same kind, in the same regime.

## Where the Arrow Is Not

The earlier film rested its arrow on one property of relative entropy: it is not symmetric. The divergence of one state from a second is not, in general, the divergence of the second from the first. That is true. It is an asymmetry between the two things being compared. It says nothing about earlier and later.

The bound uses relative entropy for a different property, its positivity, and positivity holds at every instant, for every state, run forwards or backwards. Casini gave positivity its physical reading, and the reading is about equilibrium, not change: “the positivity of the relative entropy is equivalent to the fact that the free energy of the thermal equilibrium state is the minimum one”.

For the area bound, its author made the absence of an arrow his central point.

SOURCE. In fact the covariant entropy bound does not even refer to "future" and "past." The conjecture is manifestly time reversal invariant. We regard this as its most significant property. After all, thermodynamic entropy is never T-invariant, and neither is the generalized second law of thermodynamics, which underlies Bekenstein's bound.

From this Bousso drew the conclusion that the bound's origin "cannot be thermodynamic, but must be statistical", since “the very concept of thermodynamic entropy has a built-in arrow of time” and the bound has none.

Relative entropy does carry a direction elsewhere in this literature, through a different property. Restrict two states to a smaller set of observables and they become harder to tell apart, so their relative entropy cannot rise. Edward Witten's review separates the two uses: positivity serves the Bekenstein bound, and "the more subtle property of monotonicity of relative entropy" serves the generalised second law. In 2011 Aron Wall used monotonicity to prove that law for quantum fields falling across a horizon. There the total of the entropy outside and the entropy of the horizon must increase as time passes. The direction comes from the setting: a causal horizon, defined as the boundary of the past of any future infinite worldline, and a fixed reference state. It does not come from the asymmetry of the divergence.

There is one place where relative entropy measures the arrow of time directly. In 2009 Juan Parrondo, Christian Van den Broeck and Ryoichi Kawai showed that the entropy a process produces equals “the relative entropy between forward and backward states”, the distinguishability of a process from its own time reverse. That result starts from given initial conditions, and the initial conditions are where the standard account of the arrow lives. The laws of mechanics run equally well in reverse, which is Loschmidt's objection of 1876. The asymmetry has to come from temporally asymmetric boundary conditions, or from laws that are not time-reversal invariant. The first route is the Past Hypothesis, David Albert's name for the posit that the universe began in an extremely tiny section of its available phase space. It has critics. John Earman argued that it cannot be stated in the language of general relativity, and concluded that it isn't even coherent enough to be false.

Breath on a cold pane fades in one direction and never gathers back. That is the ordinary arrow: water evaporating into air drier than itself, in a universe that began in an unusually ordered state. The earlier film made the fading pane the image of the bound. The bound has no part in it.

## Where the Observer Is

The earlier film's second addition was the observer, and here the physics gives it something real, in a different place.

The algebra of observables for a region of quantum field theory is of the kind mathematicians call type III. It has no trace, so no density matrix, and no von Neumann entropy. That is the rigorous form of the infinity Casini subtracted. It does not mean the region holds no information. The relative entropy between two states of the region is still defined, and it is the quantity the bound uses. The crossed product of 2023 is a regulator for quantum field theory without gravity. In the paper's own argument the word observer does not occur.

Observers enter when gravity does. In 2021 Witten found that, for a black hole in anti-de Sitter space, including the first corrections beyond the limit of a very large number of fields turns the algebra into one that has a trace, so that the black hole's entropy becomes defined up to an additive constant. In 2022 Venkatesa Chandrasekaran, Longo, Geoff Penington and Witten described the observables of a static patch of de Sitter space, the kind of space a universe with a positive cosmological constant approaches.

SOURCE. We describe an algebra of observables for a static patch in de Sitter space, with operators gravitationally dressed to the worldline of an observer. … There is a natural notion of entropy for a state of such an algebra.

The algebra is of type two, and it has a trace: including the observer is what makes an entropy definable there. The finding that followed points away from the earlier film's conclusion. In 2024 De Vuyst and three colleagues let several possible observers be present at once.

SOURCE. We find that the entropies seen by distinct observers can drastically differ. Our work makes extensive use of the formalism of quantum reference frames (QRF); indeed, as we point out, the 'observers' considered here and in the previous works are nothing but QRFs.

So where an observer is genuinely adjoined, in gravity, the entropy depends on which observer it is, and the observer is a quantum reference frame. The older result of Marolf, Minic and Ross makes a related point: an accelerated observer and an inertial one assign an object near a horizon radically different entropies. Taken together, the results make entropy relative to a frame in gravitational settings. None of them says that a surface has no entropy until an observer arrives, or that the observer brings information into being.

## What Is Declined

This film declines the arrow on the surface. The Bekenstein bound, in Casini's form, is a positivity statement that holds in both directions of time, and its author describes the covariant bound as time-reversal invariant. Where relative entropy carries a direction, the direction is supplied by a horizon, a reference state or an initial condition.

It declines the observer who completes the proof. The relative entropy at the bound's core was defined without one, the 2018 and 2023 proofs adjoin none, and the observers of de Sitter space give entropies that change with the observer.

It declines the claim that an earlier essay in this collection predicted Casini's result. That essay was written in June 2026, eighteen years after the result it was said to foresee.

It declines the inference from the bound to folding. Lungs, cortex and soil fold for reasons of their own, and none of them comes near ten to the sixty-nine bits per square metre.

It declines the word hologram in the sense of a part containing the whole. In physics the word names data written on a boundary, and Bousso set the optical sense aside in a footnote.

It keeps the correction the earlier film made: the Bekenstein bound limits entropy by energy and size, and the area belongs to the black-hole equality and the holographic bounds. It keeps Casini's result as what it is, a short proof that in quantum field theory the bound costs nothing extra. Whether a fold, a monad and a Markov blanket name one structure is a question the collection asks in its own voice, in other films. This one leaves it there.
