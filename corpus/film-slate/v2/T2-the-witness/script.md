# The Witness

## The Station Master

In a lecture at the Indian Institute of Technology in Kanpur in 2014, Swami Sarvapriyananda of the Ramakrishna Order described a station master. An express train pulls in, crowded, and people get on and off in a rush, and the station master watches, gives the signal, and the train leaves. He does not leave with it. A goods train comes, smoky, with one or two people, and goes, and he stays. Then there is no train at all, only an empty platform, and he is still there, watching.

The picture belongs to a doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, the non-dual school of Indian philosophy. The doctrine is the sakshin, the witness. It makes four claims, and they are worth keeping apart. Consciousness is distinct from the mind and everything the mind contains. It is self-luminous: it reveals itself without needing a second consciousness to reveal it. It runs unchanged through waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep. And it is one, not one per person.

Sarvapriyananda's lecture was the source of one of this slate's earlier films, and two essays, on self-luminous knowing and on direct verification, were the sources of two more. On the witness itself the first followed him closely. Around it, the three dated the tradition to three thousand years, offered the edge of sleep as the place to meet the witness, and answered a critic who had been misread, and none of those claims survived a second reading of the sources. Here the doctrine is given at its strongest, in its own texts, and then the answers it has met, which are older and stronger than those films allowed.

## The Light When the Lights Go Out

The witness has a long prehistory in the Upanishads. In the Brihadaranyaka, King Janaka of Videha questions the sage Yajnavalkya. What light does a person have? The sun, says Yajnavalkya. And when the sun has set? The moon. And when the moon has set? Fire. And when the fire has gone out? Speech: in the dark, where one cannot see one's own hands, one walks towards a raised voice. Janaka asks once more.

SOURCE. But when the sun has set, Yajnavalkya, and the moon has set, and the fire has gone out, and speech is hushed, what light does a person here have?

SOURCE. The soul (atman), indeed, is his light

That is Robert Ernest Hume's translation of 1921. A few verses later the text says where this light shows itself most plainly. In sleep, the person takes the world apart and builds it again, and dreams by his own brightness. "Then this person becomes self-illuminated." The first place the Upanishad calls the self self-illuminated is the dream. In dreamless sleep, it says, the seer still sees, though there is nothing to see: "there is no cessation of the seeing of a seer, because of his imperishability".

The same tradition recorded the first objection. In the Chandogya, the god Indra is taught that the self is the one who sleeps without dreaming. He sets off content, and on the way he stops.

SOURCE. Assuredly, indeed, this one does not exactly know himself (atmanam) with the thought "I am he," nor indeed the things here. He becomes one who has gone to destruction. I see nothing enjoyable in this.

Whether anything is aware in dreamless sleep was an open question inside the Upanishads before it became a quarrel between schools.

## The Fourth

The Mandukya Upanishad, twelve short verses, gives the analysis its standard form. Waking, dream and deep sleep are three quarters of the self, and there is a fourth. The earlier films called this a three-thousand-year-old text. Scholars disagree about its date, and none of them puts it there. Patrick Olivelle writes that it "cannot be much older than the beginning of the common era"; Hajime Nakamura and Richard King place it in the first two centuries of the common era; one encyclopedia entry puts it in the fifth or fourth century before. Its first great commentator, Gaudapada, worked in the sixth century, and Shankara, the school's master, around the eighth.

The verse on the fourth is a run of negations. In Swami Nikhilananda's translation it begins:

SOURCE. Turīya is not that which is conscious of the internal (subjective) world, nor that which is conscious of the external (objective) world, nor that which is conscious of both, nor that which is a mass all sentiency, nor that which is simple consciousness, nor that which is insentient.

Turiya is simply the word for fourth. The verse itself uses caturtham, the ordinary ordinal; the older Upanishads already have turiya and turya.

Each negation removes a state. Shankara explains the third: by saying the fourth is not conscious of both, "it is denied that Turīya is any intermediate state between the waking and the dream states". Sarvapriyananda made the same point in his lecture, with a joke about the notes students write as they drift off at the end of a long class. Two of the earlier films did the opposite. They offered the moment of falling asleep, aware but of nothing in particular, as the place where the witness is met directly, and one of them said the Mandukya names it. The Mandukya names it in order to exclude it.

Nakamura noticed something else about these negations. Several of the words in the verse, unseen, ungraspable, without distinctive mark, unthinkable, the stilling of elaboration, are common in Mahayana Buddhist texts. Other scholars, Michael Comans among them, deny that a text teaching a permanent Self could have taken its doctrine from Buddhists. The vocabulary, at least, was shared.

## The Lamp That Needs No Lamp

Shankara's own case begins with a division. In the preface to his commentary on the Brahma Sutras, in George Thibaut's translation of 1890, the subject and the object are as different as darkness and light.

SOURCE. It is a matter not requiring any proof that the object and the subject whose respective spheres are the notion of the 'Thou' (the Non-Ego) and the 'Ego,' and which are opposed to each other as much as darkness and light are, cannot be identified.

Yet people mix them constantly, laying the attributes of one on the other, the way one takes mother-of-pearl for silver, or sees one moon as two. His example of the mistake is the sky: "non-discerning men superimpose on the ether, which is not the object of sensuous perception, dark-blue colour." Sarvapriyananda, twelve centuries later, explains the witness with the blue of the sky, and the image has this ancestor.

The argument for self-luminosity is a regress. If consciousness had to be revealed by a further consciousness, that one would need a third, and so on without end. So consciousness reveals itself. Shankara puts it as a lamp, in Swami Jagadananda's translation of his Thousand Teachings.

SOURCE. Just as a second lamp is not necessary in order to illumine a lamp, so, a second consciousness is not necessary to make known Pure Consciousness which is of the nature of the Self.

The same work argues from the witness's distinctness to its oneness. The witness of my intellect is the witness of every other intellect, because difference belongs to what is witnessed, not to the witness: "So there cannot be more than one witness."

A later manual turned all this into a practice. The Drig-Drishya-Viveka, the discrimination of the seer from the seen, is credited to Shankara in some manuscripts. Its translator Nikhilananda judged it most likely the work of Bharati Tirtha, who headed the monastery at Sringeri in the fourteenth century. It opens with a chain.

SOURCE. The form is perceived and the eye is its perceiver. It (eye) is perceived and the mind is its perceiver. The mind with its modifications is perceived and the Witness (the Self) is verily the perceiver. But It (the Witness) is not perceived (by any other).

Its method is plain: treat desire and the other states of mind as objects, and "Meditate on Consciousness as their Witness." The station master is a modern picture of the same instruction.

## The Other Answers

The doctrine was argued, against rivals who had answers of their own.

The Nyaya school held that a cognition reveals its object and not itself. It is known, when it is known at all, by a later cognition, an apperception. Stephen Phillips and Anand Vaidya describe it as "a second-level cognition that has another cognition as its object without itself being self-aware". The regress does not follow, because not every cognition needs certifying. The fourth-century commentator Vatsyayana put it practically: if knowing required an endless chain of knowing, "then everyday action and discourse would not go on". On dreamless sleep, Nyaya held that consciousness is a quality the self has only when the senses and mind are working, and that it is absent in deep sleep. The waking report, I slept peacefully and knew nothing, is on this view an inference from how one feels, not a memory.

Buddhist philosophers took the other side of the regress argument and kept the result without the witness. For Dharmakirti and others, each cognition reveals itself and its object at once. Neil Dalal, writing on Shankara, grants that their view is close to Advaita "but endeavors to be more parsimonious by eliding the witnessing consciousness". Shankara's reply was that a cognition cannot take itself as an object, as a knife cannot cut itself. In the twentieth century Jean-Paul Sartre reached the Buddhist structure from a different direction: "a consciousness has no need at all of a reflecting [higher-order] consciousness in order to be conscious of itself". Self-luminosity, it turns out, can be had without a single seer behind it.

The Yoga system kept the seer and multiplied it. Its seer is pure seeing, untouched by the mind it looks on, and liberation is the seer's separation from that mind, in James Haughton Woods' translation of 1914, "the Isolation of the Seer". But the world does not stop for one seer's freedom, because it is common to others. There is one seer per person.

And the oldest answer is the Buddha's. In a sutta of the Middle Length Discourses, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation, a monk named Sati has come to a view.

SOURCE. As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, it is just this consciousness that runs and wanders on [from birth to birth], not another.

The Buddha rebukes him. Consciousness arises from conditions, and is named by them, as a fire is named by its fuel: a wood fire, a grass fire, a fire of chaff or rubbish. Against the station master who stays while every train goes, the sutta sets a fire that is only ever the fire of what is burning.

## The Fridge Door

One modern critic appears in the Kanpur lecture. Sarvapriyananda described the psychologist Susan Blackmore's image of the refrigerator light: open the door and the light is on, and you cannot tell whether it stays on when the door is shut. He took her to be saying that the sense of a permanent I disappears when we look for it, and he answered that what disappears is the ego, the light of the waking self, while consciousness, the light that shines on that I, remains. The earlier film repeated this as a decisive reply.

Blackmore's own paper, published in 2002, uses the image for something else.

SOURCE. The modern equivalent is the metaphor of the fridge door. Is the light always on inside the fridge? You may keep opening the door, as quickly as you can, but you can never catch it out – every time you open it, the light is on.

The fridge is the stream of consciousness itself. Her students spent days asking themselves "Am I conscious now?", and found that they always seemed to be conscious when they asked, but grew "less and less sure about whether they were conscious a moment before". The argument is aimed at an inference: that awareness is always there because whenever we look, it is there. One of the earlier films made that inference its central exercise. Try to find a moment in which awareness was absent, it said; you cannot, because any absence would have been registered. Blackmore's answer is that the check is what switches the light on.

The same inference carries the doctrine through dreamless sleep, and here the best modern reader of the debate is careful on both sides. Evan Thompson takes the Advaita argument seriously enough to reject the neuroscientific habit of defining consciousness as whatever vanishes in deep sleep. Shankara's point stands, in Dalal's statement of it: "one cannot claim direct perception of unconsciousness in deep sleep". But Thompson adds that even a genuine memory of sleep "seems compatible with there having been moments or periods during which consciousness vanishes completely". The waking report does not settle what happened in the night.

## A Model of Being Awake

The evidence the earlier films offered for the witness was first-person: the felt certainty that awareness is present and unbroken. Thomas Metzinger's research on minimal phenomenal experience, published in 2020, collects meditators' reports of pure awareness and asks what they are reports of.

SOURCE. One empirical hypothesis is that the phenomenological prototype of "pure awareness", to which all such reports refer, really is the content of a predictive model, namely, a Bayesian representation of tonic alertness.

Tonic alertness is a functional state of the organism, its sustained wakefulness, and on Metzinger's account an organism can be alert without knowing that it is. On his hypothesis the experience of pure awareness is a model of that state. A model can be wrong. Some such experiences, he writes, "could be misrepresentations of tonic alertness", and the sense of knowing that comes with them "can exist without there being knowledge in the epistemological sense". One of the earlier films said that in this domain recognition is the verification, and that "There is no other kind". A misrepresentation that is not experienced as one would feel exactly the same.

Neither of these is the last word. The philosopher Miri Albahari defends witness-consciousness in analytic terms, as "mode-neutral awareness with intrinsic phenomenal character", against exactly this kind of deflation. Thompson keeps more than he removes. He grants a minimal subjectivity that may be present even in dreamless sleep, and proposes that "it is open to us today to" treat it as the embodied self, the body's own subjectivity, rather than a changeless witness. His book describes the self as "a changing process, not a static thing".

On this reading the station master is a good picture of how experience presents itself. Whether he is also a true picture of what experience is, a single, changeless, bodiless watcher, is the question the doctrine answers and its rivals deny.

## What Is Declined

The edge of sleep is not where the witness is found. The Mandukya excludes the state between waking and dream, and Shankara says so.

The text is not three thousand years old. It is most often dated to around the beginning of the common era, and its great commentators wrote between the sixth and eighth centuries.

Agreement among these traditions is not independent confirmation. Advaita and Mahayana shared vocabulary and argued with each other, and Gaudapada's verses are widely read as Buddhist-influenced; traditions in contact are not independent witnesses. Nor does recognition verify itself, and Blackmore's fridge was never about the ego.

Some smaller details fall away too. The source of the earlier film was not four lectures by Sarvapriyananda but one lecture, two short clips and a talk by Philip Goldberg in which he does not speak. A speech recogniser's garbling of a Hindi phrase was quoted as a king's words. Sarvapriyananda's paraphrase of Swami Vivekananda was quoted as Vivekananda's own sentence, which in Chicago in 1893 ran "from truth to truth, from lower to higher truth". And autexousion, a Greek word for free will, was given as a self-authenticating ground.

What remains is the doctrine in its own words, and the practice as an instruction anyone can try: treat what passes through the mind as objects, and attend to what they appear to. What that attention finds, a single witness, a momentary awareness, or a model of being awake, is still argued, as it was in the Upanishads.
